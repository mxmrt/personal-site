Why candy

Candy is the practice round. Insurance filings are the real target.

The method we're testing finds products that don't exist yet.

It places every existing product in one shared space, measures where that space is crowded and where it's empty, then writes a concrete product for an empty spot and measures it again to check that it landed there.

We want to run it on insurance filings: the policy forms, endorsements and rating plans insurers file with regulators. Before trusting it there, we ran it somewhere every step can be checked with your mouth. Most candy attributes are things you can taste or touch, so when the method makes a mistake, you can see it.

Where the idea came from

It started with 189,495 video games.

The method comes from RE:Skob's Idea Plotter, shown in a video on X in October 2026. It turned every Steam store page into a list of 1,024 numbers, so each game became a point in one shared space.

From there, crowding is simple geometry. Idea Plotter blended related games, pushed the blend into empty space, had Claude write a store page for the spot, and then measured that page again instead of trusting it.

One prompt, two AI teams

We wrote one brief and gave it, unchanged, to Claude and to OpenAI's Codex.

The 2,500-word brief laid out nine stages: restate the plan, design the attributes, build the catalog, score every candy twice, map, find empty space, write new candies, verify and calibrate them, and write a final report. Each AI worked alone, in its own workspace, and wrote a one-page report at every stage. Five rules ran through all of it.

Same ruler for old and new

New candies are written up exactly like real ones and scored by a blind scorer who never sees names or targets.

The typical gap

Measure how far each real candy sits from its closest real twin. The middle value is the typical gap. A new candy only counts as new if it sits further than that from every real candy.

No mush

Blend unrelated candies and you get a vague middle that's far from everything because it's nothing in particular.

The leash

The farthest corners of the space are nonsense. Every new candy stays close enough to the real candies it came from to be makeable and wanted.

Demand is a layer

Popularity says which neighborhoods matter. It never moves a point.

The finding that matters most for insurance

A ruler is only as steady as whoever holds it. In small tests, when one team's scorers used the other team's rubric, they disagreed by more than that rubric's own noise. Codex's later checks found the same drift with fresh scorers, even on its own rubric. So a "copy" or "new" call is only as good as the calibration of whoever made it, and every reviewer, human or model, needs its own measured noise.

The ruler

How far apart are two candies?

Every attribute gives a difference between 0 (the same) and 1 (opposite ends of its scale). Average them within each family of attributes (texture, taste, price and so on), then take a weighted average of the families, and you get one distance. Zero means identical.

This is Gower distance, a standard way to compare mixed data like "hardness 1–7" next to "contains nuts: yes or no". Context (when and where it's eaten) and commercial attributes (like price) count half as much as the rest.

distance = Σ weight × |a − b| ÷ range

The map

Every dot is a real candy.

404 candies, each scored on 65 attributes. A standard method called UMAP flattens those 65 measurements onto the page so that each candy's nearest neighbors stay near it.

It's a picture of neighborhoods, not a ruler: distances between far-apart dots get bent. Every number on this page is measured in the full space, never on the picture.

Families

The families found themselves.

Start with every candy on its own and keep merging the two closest groups. Claude used Ward's method, which prefers compact groups, and stopped at 8 families.

In Claude's first run, a method that looks only for dense clusters left nearly half the catalog unassigned. Candy is mostly a continuum with a few dense pockets.

Empty space

Some of the map is empty.

25 spots looked empty on this picture. Checked across all 65 attributes, 11 stayed empty. Those are the rings.

But the candies around each ring have little in common, so filling one would mean blending unrelated candies into mush. On a stricter test that also asks for related neighbors, none qualified, so the new candies were aimed elsewhere.

New candies

Thirty new candies, written for the gaps.

26 were blends of two to four related candies, pushed toward empty space. The other 4 came from a search that changed eight attributes of a single real candy. Each was written up like a real candy and scored blind.

Filled diamonds are tier A: still new under every stress test, with no look-alike product found (23 of 30).

Loneliness

Now measure how lonely each candy is.

For every real candy, find the distance to its nearest neighbor. Lined up by that distance, half of them sit left of the dashed line. That middle value, the median, is the typical gap, and it becomes the unit: 1×.

Measuring new

A new candy has to be lonelier than a typical real one.

All 30 new candies sit beyond the typical gap. The median new candy is 1.60× away from its nearest real candy, and 86% of real candies have a closer twin.

How a candy is born

Six steps from an empty spot to a candy you could describe to a factory.

1Pick related seeds

Two to four candies from the same or a neighboring neighborhood. Unrelated seeds make mush.

2Blend them

Average their 65 scores into one point between them.

3Push into empty space

Move the blend away from every real candy, toward the emptiest nearby spot.

4Keep it on a leash

Stop before it stops making sense. Corners of the space are things nobody could make or would want.

5Write the candy

Turn the point into a recipe, an eating moment and a pack, written up in the same seven-part template as the real candies.

6Re-measure it blind

A fresh scorer who never sees the target scores the description. It must sit further than the typical gap from every real candy, and it should land near where it was aimed. Misses are reported, not hidden.

What carries over to insurance filings

Four lessons we're taking with us.

1

Calibrate every scorer

The same rubric in two sets of hands disagreed by more than its own noise, and fresh Codex scorers drifted even on Codex's own rubric. Every reviewer or model that codes a filing needs its own measured noise, or "new" and "copy" calls mean little.

2

Check gaps in the full space, and check the neighbors

Most of what looked empty on the picture wasn't, and the gaps that were real sat between unrelated candies. A gap in a map of filed products counts only if it's empty across every coverage attribute and surrounded by related products.

3

New, on target and good are three different things

A candy can be far from everything and still miss its target or fail in a factory. For a filing, novelty starts the underwriting conversation. It doesn't finish it.

4

The leash becomes price and sell

In candy the leash is "could a factory make it, would anyone buy it". In insurance it's "can it be priced, filed and sold", and demand comes from premium and exposure instead of popularity.
